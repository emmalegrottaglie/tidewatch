How it works

Tidewatch reads a community's or account's recent posts straight from the platform, notes the hour each one went up, and compares how posts from each hour of the week did. Everything happens in your browser: there is no Tidewatch server, no account and no tracking.

Why Reddit uses an archive: since 2026, reddit.com refuses requests from signed-out browsers and sends no cross-origin headers, so no static website can read it directly. Arctic Shift records each post seconds after it appears and reads it again about 36 hours later, updating its upvotes and comment count. Tidewatch only scores posts older than two days, so every score comes from that second reading.

Very busy subreddits publish more posts than can be read in one go. For those, Tidewatch reads one page of posts every two hours across four weeks and says so on the results.

Popular subreddits are also read once a day by a scheduled job and saved with the site, so they open instantly. The results say when that copy was made and offer to read the subreddit live instead.

The Bluesky benchmark

No public API lists every post on Bluesky with its likes, so Tidewatch measures it. Every hour, a scheduled job listens to Bluesky's public firehose for five minutes, counts every new post that isn't a reply, and keeps a random sample of 100. Two days later it reads those posts again through the public Bluesky API to get their final likes, reposts, replies and quotes. Over a few weeks every hour of the week is sampled on several days, and the last 28 days are kept. Posts are grouped by the first language their author set.

It answers a different question from analyzing one account: when posts from everyone tend to do well. Your own audience may keep different hours, so analyze your account too.

Instagram, TikTok, X, Facebook, LinkedIn, Threads and YouTube don't offer public data a website can read without a server and a login, so for those you bring the export from your own analytics.

How each hour is scored

  1. Posts younger than 24 hours (two days on Reddit) are left out, because they are still collecting interactions. Reddit's AutoModerator threads are left out too, since they are posted on a schedule.
  2. Each post is ranked against all the others by the metric you pick. A post that beat 90% of them scores 90%; ties share the difference. Ranks are graded, so a huge post counts for more than a decent one, but no post can score above 100%, so one viral post can't decide the answer. They also work the same whether typical posts get 1 like or 10,000, which matters on Reddit, where most posts sit at 1 upvote.
  3. Ranks and post counts are added up for each of the 168 hours of the week, in the time zone you pick, then smoothed with the neighbouring hours (a quarter from each side), because a post at 4:59 and one at 5:01 are really the same choice.
  4. Each hour's score is the average rank of its posts, pulled toward 50% as if it had three extra average posts. An hour with one lucky post doesn't beat an hour with twenty good ones.
  5. A score of 50% is a typical hour. 65% means posts published then beat 65% of the other posts, on average.

The activity view counts events per hour instead (comments for Reddit, published posts elsewhere), divided by how many times each hour of the week appears in the period read, so a window with two Tuesdays and one Wednesday doesn't make Tuesday look twice as busy.

When fewer than 15 posts can be scored, the recommendations come from activity instead, and the page says which one it used.

What it can't tell you

These are patterns in past posts, not a promise. A good hour for a community is partly a good hour because people already post then; what you post matters more than when. Small accounts produce noisy results, and the page warns when the sample is small. Counts on federated platforms (Mastodon, Lemmy) only include what the server you query has heard about.
